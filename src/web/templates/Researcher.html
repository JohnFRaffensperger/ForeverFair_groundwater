Researcher overview to Forever Fair

This page gives you god-like single entry point to all application pages and documentation. The software is not designed for security but rather for explanation. Adding security is probably pretty easy to vibe-code in.

Forever Fair is a periodic groundwater allocation auction. Each auction covers one or more delivery periods. A catchment manager sets up each auction, opens it for bidding, runs the clearing model, and publishes results. Traders log in, submit or revise bids, and view their accepted quantities and prices. Traders who hold water rights submit bids stating how much water they want to buy or sell in each period, and at what price. The system then clears the market: it solves an optimization model that accepts or rejects bids to maximise the total value of trade while keeping pumping within the environmental limits set by the hydrologist.

The market design puts environmental limits first. The hydrologist sets hard bounds on head at control points around the catchment. These bounds cannot be overridden by high bids. The catchment has control points where head must be constrained, such as to ensure a wetland does not run dry. The model must have a head limit for each control point. You can choose to include some or all wells in the list of control points to make sure those wells have sufficient head.

Technically, you should start with your groundwater model. From the groundwater model, GWM2K (or similar) can create a linear program file in MPS format. This MPS file includes the response matrix, head constraints, and decision variables. It sets up the main structure of the auction clearing model. This eases setting up the trading system! The hydrologist is responsible for ensuring the validity of the response matrix. Participants take water from one or more wells.

Legal authority

Legally, the auction manager must have legal authority to run this auction. The auction manager must have legal authority to prescribe participants' rights.

Users have to agree to upgrade their water rights from vague quantities per season to detailed quantities per day or per week. In return, they can change these quantities easily through the market and they will be relieved of perverse rules like "use it or lose it".

Users must agree to let the auction manager scale their allocations to actual available water. Users must not take more water than the auction manager allocates to them. Users' wells must have real time meters that the auction manager can see.

Users have to learn to use the website and learn to make bids. The auction manager must be sure that users can and will pay.

Users must agree to abide by market rules. They must give up the right to dispute allocations. Instead, they should dispute the hydrology model and optimization model.

Lawmakers must require the same of all other stakeholders: stakeholders must lose the right to dispute allocations, and to have long periods of review and public comment. Instead, all stakeholders should have visibility of and rights to dispute the hydrology model and optimization model.

Rights.

Users participate voluntarily, but they must agree to upgrade their water rights from vague quantities per season to detailed quantities per day or per week. Rights must follow the auction schedule, because prices will follow the auction schedule. That is, everyone always pretended that one ML of water taken at the beginning of the season was the same as one ML of water taken in the middle of the season. We all know that is not true. The market distinguishes those different periods by different rights and different prices. In return, they can change these quantities easily through the market rather than go through a laborious application process.

For example, suppose a user has a stipulated right to take 140 ML per season over 20 weeks. When upgrading, the rights should probably change to 140/20 = 7ML per week.

Water allocated for the immediate period is real water. The auction manager must commit to letting people take that water. Because users can sell water they don't expect to need, they are likely to take all the water they have allocated.

Water scheduled for future periods is subject to scaling, because inflows may not match the forecast. For example, suppose a user has a right of 7ML for each week, and in week 4 buys 3 more ML for week 9, resulting in a tentative right of 10 ML for week 9. When week 9 actually arrives in a surprise drought, the hydrologist might update the heads, thus requiring everyone to be scaled by 70%. So the user's right of 10 ML becomes 7 ML. This gives users an incentive to pay attention to the weather and to buy only what they need.

The market design can easily support other types of rights, e.g., seniority rights where the auction manager allocates initial rights first to the most senior users. See Raffensperger, "Matching users' rights to available groundwater."

Auction operation

Auctions run on a regular schedule. The response matrix and groundwater behavior imply an auction schedule. Each auction trades water for one or more periods, days or weeks, which the hydrologist can choose. A period is the time denominator for traded water, e.g., ML/day or ML/week. For example, an auction cleared today might allow trading of water for the next four weeks.

An optional tentative run before closing lets traders see likely outcomes and adjust their bids.

The market provides location-specific prices. Because pumping at one location affects water levels elsewhere, the clearing price can differ from well to well and from period to period.

The hydrologist can update the head constraints to manage forecasted inflows.

The market clearing linear program is gross pool: decision variables are non-negative, representing total accepted quantity, not net trades. Bids represent total desired holdings, not buy/sell increments relative to initial allocations. Settlement is post-solution: database table TraderPeriodResult records both accepted_quantity and initial_allocation separately, so the buy/sell netting happens outside the LP, not inside the model. The advantage is that the LP should always be feasible, as zero is always a valid solution.
